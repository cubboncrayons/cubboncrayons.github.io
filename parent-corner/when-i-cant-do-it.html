“I Can't Do It!” — What Your Child Really Needs in That Moment

When a child gives up, help them face frustration without doing everything for them.

By Cubbon Crayons · Published 22 November 2026

Four-year-old Appi is building a tower with colourful blocks. One block, two blocks, three blocks. As she places the fourth, the tower tumbles down. She tries again. It falls again. “Ajja, I can't do it!”

Ajja could rebuild it in seconds. Instead he sits beside her: “You worked hard on that. It's disappointing when it falls, isn't it?” Appi nods. “Shall we take a break? Or would you like help with the first block?” She reaches for a block. He stays nearby without taking over.

A small tower has fallen. But an opportunity to build confidence remains.

When helping becomes doing everything

We tie shoelaces, finish puzzles and supply answers before children have finished thinking, often because we're in a hurry or want to help. Yet children also need opportunities to learn what they can accomplish themselves.

In a Bengaluru parenting programme reported by Prajavani, educationist Dr. Gururaj Karajagi discussed the pitfalls of parents doing work that children could gradually learn to do themselves. This is a paraphrase of newspaper reporting, not a verbatim quotation. [1]

Helping a child grow is not the same as doing everything for them.

Find words to try

What does “I can't” mean?

The same words can mean “I don't know how”, “I'm tired”, “I'm afraid I'll fail again”, or “I need help”. Before insisting on another attempt, ask: “Which part is difficult?”, “Would you like some help?” or “Do you need a break?”

Four things to try

  1. Recognise the feeling

    “You wanted it to work. It's upsetting when it doesn't.” Understanding frustration does not mean removing every challenge.

  2. Make the task smaller

    “Instead of six blocks, shall we try two?” Let the child experience a manageable step.

  3. Help without taking over

    “I can hold the bottom block while you place the next one.” Offer only as much assistance as needed.

  4. Notice a specific action

    “You tried a wider block this time.” Specific feedback is more useful than praising only a result. A break is also fine when a child is overwhelmed.

Tonight's activity: The Wobbly Tower

Take six blocks. Ask your child to build a tower and, if it falls, resist immediately rebuilding it yourself.

Words you can try

“What could we change?”

“Would you like a hint, or another try?”

For ages 2–3, try two or three blocks. Ages 4–6 can vary the base. Ages 7–10 can compare the stability of different structures. It is an exploration, not a test.

A thought to carry home

Today it may be a block tower; tomorrow it may be a bicycle, and later a bigger problem. Children shouldn't be expected to face every difficulty alone, but they need room to try.

When your child says “I can't”, ask: “Which part can we try together?”
