Sharing & visiting family · Ages 2–5

My child won’t share a favourite toy. Am I getting this wrong?

When a small disagreement starts to feel like a judgement on your parenting, it helps to pause and look at the moment differently.

By Cubbon Crayons · Sources checked 21 November 2026

First article · Editorial pilot

In this answer

  • What informed this answer

Illustrative situation, not a reader submission. Original editorial guidance informed by the sources below; not an individual assessment.

You wanted a happy visit with the cousins. Instead, your child is holding tightly to one toy, someone is waiting, and you can feel the room watching. It can leave you wondering whether this says something about your parenting. You can help with the disagreement without turning it into a judgement about either of you.

First, take the pressure down

You can respect a treasured toy and still help your child practise taking turns. Before the next visit, choose together what to put away and what to make available. During a disagreement, stay nearby and help both children find a way forward. [1, 2]

One moment is not the whole story

A two-year-old and a five-year-old may need different help. Younger toddlers often struggle to wait; preschoolers can understand more and still find a turn difficult. Adjust your expectations to the child and the situation, rather than treating one visit as a test of kindness. [1, 2]

Make the next visit a little easier

  • Before guests arrive

    Invite your child to choose a few toys for playing together. Keep a particularly precious toy somewhere safe for the visit. [2, 3]

  • When both children want it

    Name the difficulty briefly. Help arrange a next turn or offer another activity while they wait. Step in to stop grabbing or hurting. [1, 2]

  • After everyone settles

    Practise an exchange during ordinary play, with something neither child is worried about losing. Notice an offered turn without comparing the children. [1, 2]

A LITTLE HELP WITH THE WORDS

Words you could try

Our own example phrases. Choose what feels natural; the words are not a guaranteed script.

“Shall we choose something you both want to play with?”

“You’re using this. Let’s work out the next turn together.”

And if a relative comments?

You do not have to explain your whole approach while the children are upset. You could say: “I’ll help them with this. We can talk afterwards.” This is an example response, not a rule for every family.

If it is still difficult

Try a shorter visit or a different activity. If disagreements repeatedly become unsafe or feel unmanageable, ask a qualified professional for guidance suited to your family. There is no need to label your child from this article.
