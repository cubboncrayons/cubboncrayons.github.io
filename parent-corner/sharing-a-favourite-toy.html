Sharing & visiting family · Ages 2–5

My child won’t share a favourite toy. Am I getting this wrong?

One toy, two children—and that uncomfortable feeling that everyone is watching. Let’s make the next moment a little easier.

By Cubbon Crayons · Sources checked 21 November 2026

First article · Editorial pilot

You hoped the cousins would have a lovely time together. Now your child is holding tightly to one toy, another child is waiting, and you’re wondering what everyone thinks. It can be an awkward moment. It doesn’t have to become a judgement on you or your child.

You don’t have to solve it all today

Your child can practise taking turns without having to hand over every treasured toy. Put a special toy away before a visit and choose some others to play with together. If an argument starts, stay nearby and help. You don’t have to teach it all in one afternoon. [1, 2]

Find words to try

Waiting can still be hard

A two-year-old and a five-year-old may need different help. Younger toddlers often find waiting hard. Preschoolers may understand taking turns and still struggle when it is their favourite toy. Start with the help your child needs in this moment; one difficult visit is not a test of kindness. [1, 2]

Try this at the next visit

  • Before everyone arrives

    Ask your child to help choose a few toys for playing together. Put a particularly precious toy somewhere safe for the visit. This gives you something to agree on before anyone is waiting. [2, 3]

  • When they both want the same toy

    Keep it short: both children want the same thing. Help arrange the next turn, or offer something else to do while waiting. Step in to stop grabbing or hurting. [1, 2]

  • Later, when things are calm

    Try taking turns during ordinary play, with something your child is less worried about handing over. When they offer a turn, notice it warmly, without comparing them with another child. [1, 2]

A LITTLE HELP WITH THE WORDS

Words you could try

You don’t need a perfect speech. One gentle sentence is a place to start.

“Shall we find something you can both play with?”

“You’re still playing with this. Let’s work out who has the next turn.”

And if a relative says something?

That can make an already awkward moment feel harder. You can keep your reply short: “I’ll help them. Let’s give them a little time.” You can talk more later, when the children are settled.

And if the next visit is difficult too?

You can try a shorter visit or a different activity. If disagreements repeatedly become unsafe or feel too hard to manage, ask a qualified professional for guidance suited to your family.

For now, choose one small thing to try. The rest can wait.
