When the Screen Goes Off, What Comes Next?

Sometimes the best childhood memories begin with an empty cardboard box.

By Cubbon Crayons · Published 23 November 2026

The television goes off. Appi looks at the blank screen, then at Appa. “Now what?” You wanted less screen time, but a harder question appears: what is waiting for your child on the other side of the screen? Maybe the apartment playground is crowded, the road is busy, and you have just returned from work. The screen is convenient—not because you don't care, but because you are tired.

The problem is bigger than the screen

Research and reporting from Bengaluru have examined shrinking opportunities for children's play, including local studies involving parents. These are specific findings, not proof that every Karnataka family faces the same situation. [1–3] A screen-time boundary works better as a family routine when children also have chances to move, imagine and connect.

The day Appi became a bus driver

Appa spots an empty cardboard box. “Appi, our bus is ready. But where's the driver?” “I'm the driver!” Appi announces. Her brother becomes the conductor, and Appa asks for a ticket to Ajja's house. Soon the living room is a Bengaluru street. When her brother wants to drive, Appa asks, “How can we make sure you both get a turn?” Appi decides: “I'll drive there. He can drive us back!” A small pretend game has offered a chance to practise listening and negotiating—not a guaranteed lesson, but a meaningful opportunity.

A thought from Kannada literature

In Kuvempu's children's collection Meghapura, the poem Shishya imagines an adult asking a child to teach him the joys of childhood play. This is a sourced paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation. It reminds us that parents do not always have to direct play. Sometimes a child can lead. [5]

Four things to try tonight

  1. Prepare the transition

    “When this video ends, shall we make something together?”

  2. Offer two choices

    “A bus or a pillow fort?”

  3. Follow your child's lead

    Ask where the bus goes rather than setting every rule.

  4. Hold the boundary kindly

    Acknowledge disappointment without switching the screen back on.

Words you could try

When stopping is hard

“I know you wanted more. It's hard to stop something you enjoy. The screen is finished for now. I'm here. Shall we build our own adventure?”

Tonight's activity: Our Bengaluru Bus

Take a clean cardboard box, paper tickets and pretend steering wheel; adults handle cutting and remove staples. Ages 2–4: make bus sounds and name stops. Ages 5–7: take turns and help imaginary passengers. Ages 8–10: design a route and solve pretend travel problems. Ask: “What was your favourite part of our ride?” Indoor pretend play complements, rather than replaces, physical activity. [6]

A thought to carry home

A switched-off screen creates an opening. Shared play can fill it with connection.

Little stories. Meaningful activities. Growing hearts. — Cubbon Crayons
